What might it mean to be a British Muslim today?

The big question: Britain is a diverse, multi-faith country. Islam is the second largest religion in the UK, and most British Muslims were born here and call Britain home. This organiser gathers the key facts, vocabulary and ideas you need — use it while you work, then hide it once you are confident.

British Muslims are not one single group. They trace their heritage to many parts of the world — South Asia, the Middle East, Africa, Turkey and beyond — and include people who have converted to Islam. There is huge variety in how people live out their faith.

Key words
Islam
A major world religion; the word means peace and submission to God.
Muslim
A follower of Islam — someone who submits to God (Allah).
Allah
The Arabic word for the one God, worshipped in Islam.
Qur'an
The holy book of Islam, believed to be the word of God revealed to the Prophet Muhammad.
Ummah
The worldwide community of all Muslims, united across countries and cultures.

The place where Muslims gather to pray and to serve the community.
Imam
A person who leads prayers in a mosque.
Halal
Something permitted in Islam — often used for food prepared in the allowed way.

A headscarf some Muslim women choose to wear as a sign of faith and modesty.

A society where religion and government are kept separate, and many faiths live side by side.

British and Muslim — a blended identity

Belonging

Most British Muslims feel proud to be both British and Muslim. They vote, pay taxes, follow local teams, study for GCSEs and take part in every part of national life.

Diversity

There is no single "British Muslim". Communities differ by heritage, language, and how they practise. Many are Sunni, some are Shia, and levels of observance vary widely.

Contribution

British Muslims work in the NHS, schools, business, sport, the armed forces and politics, and are known for generous charitable giving, especially during Ramadan.

Key facts
  • The 2021 Census found around 3.9 million people (about 6.5%) in England and Wales identified as Muslim — the second largest religious group.
  • A large share of British Muslims were born in the UK; for younger generations, Britain is simply home.
  • Everyday faith in Britain can mean finding a quiet space to pray, choosing halal food, or fasting while still going to school or work.
  • Eid and Ramadan are increasingly recognised in British schools, workplaces and even by public landmarks lit up to mark the occasion.
Talking about this with respect

When exploring this topic, avoid stereotypes — the idea that all Muslims are the same, or that being Muslim and being British are in conflict. Some British Muslims experience Islamophobia (prejudice or hostility because of their faith), which is unfair and inaccurate. Good religious study means using accurate words, listening to real voices, and remembering the enormous variety within any community.

Compare two of the Five Pillars — Ramadan (fasting) and Hajj (pilgrimage).
Knowledge

Ramadan is the month of fasting from dawn to sunset; Muslims focus on prayer, charity and self-discipline. Hajj is the once-in-a-lifetime pilgrimage to Makkah, made if a person is able. A British Muslim might fast during a normal school or work day, or travel from a UK airport to make Hajj.
